Research

My research sits around a few connected questions: what dark matter is, how it shapes galaxies together with ordinary baryonic physics, and how reliably any of that can be recovered from simulations and observations. The topics below are related rather than separate — most of what I do touches more than one of them.

Dark matter

I am interested in the nature of dark matter, and in what observations of galaxies and large-scale structure can actually tell us about it. It helps to think of the alternatives to cold dark matter in three broad classes: gravity-only models, such as cold or warm dark matter, where the physics is set entirely by the initial power spectrum; models with additional interactions, such as self-interacting dark matter, where short-range interactions between particles redistribute energy within haloes; and scalar-field models, such as fuzzy or axion-like dark matter, where a very light particle mass produces wave-like behaviour on astrophysical scales. Each class leaves a different signature in the structure of dark matter haloes and the galaxies that form within them, but those signatures are easily confused with the effects of baryonic feedback. Most of my work on this comes down to disentangling the two: running matched simulations in which only the dark matter physics changes, so that a genuine difference in outcome can be attributed to the dark matter model rather than absorbed into a feedback prescription. I refer to this general problem — testing dark matter across its different classes on a like-for-like basis — as extended dark matter, or xDM.

Galaxy formation and dynamics

Dark matter and baryonic physics act together to determine the structure and dynamics of galaxies. I work on questions of galaxy and halo structure, disc stability, baryonic contraction, and how simulated galaxies compare with observed ones.

Cosmological simulations

Much of this work is done with large cosmological simulations, including zoom simulations of individual haloes. I am interested both in using simulations to answer scientific questions and in the numerical and computational methods that make those calculations tractable in the first place. An early example was establishing the numerical convergence criteria used to judge whether a simulated dark matter halo's inner structure can be trusted (Power et al. 2003), which remains a standard reference in the field.

Computational astrophysics and scientific infrastructure

I like building things that other researchers can use: simulation infrastructure, scientific software, reproducible computational workflows, and tools that make complicated calculations easier to inspect and extend. Software written with my students and postdocs over the years includes halo-finding and merger-tree codes, tools for predicting the abundance of dark matter haloes, and pipelines for generating synthetic observations from simulated galaxies. This extends to high-performance and GPU computing, and to broader Australian computational-science infrastructure and community efforts — I sit on the National Computational Merit Allocation Committee and have chaired scientific HPC advisory committees for Astronomy Australia.

Scientific inference

A question that runs through most of the above is where information is lost along the chain from fundamental physics, through simulation and galaxy formation, to what we actually observe and infer. Working out which assumptions matter, and what a given observation can genuinely constrain, is as much a part of the work as any individual result.
