Projects

I am interested in projects that start with a physical question and then require some combination of theory, simulation, computation and observation to answer it. I particularly like problems where the obvious answer turns out not to be quite enough: where different pieces of physics can produce similar observations, where information has to be followed across several scales, or where answering the question requires building something that does not yet exist.

The projects below are examples of that kind of work. Some are current, some have developed over several years, and some have grown into software or broader research programmes.

Extended dark matter

I am interested in dark matter as a set of physical possibilities rather than simply as a choice between named particle models. The xDM framework is an attempt to describe those possibilities in terms of properties that matter for structure formation: wave-like or particle-like behaviour, cold or warm initial conditions, and collisional or collisionless dynamics. Different combinations of these properties leave different signatures in the formation and structure of dark matter haloes and galaxies.

The interesting question is then what can actually be learned from observations. I am exploring how observed structural properties of galaxies constrain what is possible within a given dark matter framework, while accounting for the dynamical response of the halo and the effects of galaxy formation. The aim is not simply to ask whether one model fits better than another, but to understand which physical information survives into observable structure and what constraints can genuinely be deduced from it.

From the early Universe to the Milky Way

DORCHA is a programme of cosmological zoom simulations designed to connect the physics of early galaxy formation with the galaxies and dark matter structures we can study in the local Universe. The idea is to follow the remnants of early structure formation forward into the present-day environment, where they can be compared with observations of dwarf galaxies, stellar populations and the Milky Way's halo.

This also provides a connection to the broader SOLAS simulation work, linking questions about high-redshift galaxies and the early Universe to the detailed structure of their descendants in our cosmic backyard. I find this connection between very different epochs particularly interesting: observations made at low redshift can retain information about physical processes that operated when the Universe was young.

Galaxy structure as a probe of dark matter

Galaxies provide several different ways of asking what their dark matter haloes can and cannot be doing. I am interested in using observed structural and dynamical properties of galaxies to determine which dark matter configurations are physically allowable, and in understanding how the halo responds dynamically as the galaxy forms.

This includes questions of disc stability, halo response and baryonic contraction, but the broader problem is more general: given an observed galaxy, what does its structure tell us about the dark matter physics that could have produced it? These constraints are useful precisely because they do not depend on identifying a single distinctive dark matter signature; they instead ask what combinations of physics are compatible with the structure that is actually observed.

Feedback across scales

Feedback is one of the recurring problems in galaxy formation because energy and momentum injected on relatively small scales can alter structures on much larger ones. I have worked on several forms of this problem, including AGN jets and feedback in dwarf galaxies, and on how feedback changes the relationship between galaxies and their surrounding haloes.

I am interested in feedback both as a physical process and as a source of uncertainty when galaxies are used as probes of other physics. Understanding what feedback can plausibly change is essential if a difference between two dark matter models is to be interpreted as evidence for the dark matter rather than for an imperfect model of galaxy formation.

Transient and compact-object populations

Some of my work has followed the consequences of stellar and binary evolution into the wider Universe. This includes high-mass X-ray binaries and their possible contribution to heating and ionising the intergalactic medium during reionisation, as well as work on fast radio bursts and other transient populations.

These problems appeal to me for much the same reason as the cosmological work: relatively small-scale physical processes can leave consequences that become visible on galactic or cosmological scales, provided we understand the chain connecting the two.

Building the machinery

Some projects begin with a scientific question for which the existing computational tools are simply not adequate. DORCHA, Ashvini and foraois are examples of this, as is my work on the Arepo-AI framework: developing ways of combining simulation, machine learning and physical modelling to make new classes of numerical experiment possible.

I enjoy this part of the work. The code is not the end in itself; it is a way of making an otherwise inaccessible scientific question experimentally tractable. Sometimes the most interesting result of a project is therefore not a particular simulation or prediction, but a new way of asking the question.

The kinds of problems I like

The projects I find most satisfying tend to have a few things in common. They involve a genuinely interesting physical question, connect ideas that are usually considered separately, and leave room for the answer to be surprising. I like problems where it is necessary to work out what can actually be inferred rather than simply demonstrate that a model can reproduce an observation, and I am quite happy for the answer to be that an apparently useful constraint is weaker than it first appeared.

That is probably why my research has wandered across rather different parts of astrophysics. The individual systems change, but the underlying questions tend to recur: how does physics become structure, how does structure become something observable, and how much of the physics can we still see at the end of that process?
